# Young conifer cones taper into stalks, not plinths

None of the floras, silvics manuals or phenology studies gathered for these 13 species describes a broad, flat base on a young seed cone. Every early-stage description points the other way. The Douglas fir receptive strobilus is still half-sheathed in bud scales. The giant sequoia conelet is only two to three times the diameter of its twig. Scots pine conelets are "egg-shaped" on a short stalk, and Merkus pine has a "rounded base before opening". A generator that gives young cones a broad, flat base is therefore reproducing a mature-cone feature, the swollen basal apophyses, open scales or shoulder pressed against a needle rosette, at a stage where it does not exist. The young base should taper or round into a stalk, bud-scale collar or short shoot whose thickness changes little with age. What "young" means varies widely:

- **Single-season cones** (Douglas fir, noble fir, Norway spruce, Japanese larch) are pollinated in spring and reach full size within roughly two to three months. Their "young" phase is only a few weeks long.
- **Two-year pines** (lodgepole, sugar, Scots, chir and Merkus pine) spend a full year as a small, closed egg at about 15–40% of final length, then enlarge in year two.
- **Stone pine** idles at 10–20 mm for two years before a single spring growth burst in year three.
- **Giant sequoia** is "almost full-size" within about four months of pollination.
- **Cedar of Lebanon** is pollinated in autumn and only "swells" late in its cycle.
- **Bunya pine** matures in about 17 months, but no source documents its young stages at all.

After each species reaches full size, its outline, length-to-diameter ratio (L:D), scale count, attachment and orientation hold steady. Only colour, lignification, scale opening and (for the firs, cedar and bunya) disintegration change. So middle-to-late morphology can stay as it is, provided "middle" is defined as "after full size", not as a fixed calendar fraction. No measured length-against-time growth curve was retrievable for any of the 13 species. Stage endpoints are sourced, and any curve between them is a modelling assumption.

## Stage endpoints are sourced; growth curves between them are not

The research covered silvics manuals, the USDA Woody Plant Seed Manual (WPSM), regional floras, extension guides and a handful of phenology papers. Several primary monographs were inaccessible, and those are the likely sources of real growth curves: Allen & Owens (1972) on Douglas fir, Owens & Molder (1984) on lodgepole pine, Sarvas on Scots pine and Norway spruce, the Mutke and Calama stone pine datasets, and Buchholz (1937–38) on sequoia. As a result the stage tables below rely on three kinds of value, marked consistently:

- **Plain values with a linked source** are sourced facts.
- **Values in italics marked (inf.)** are inferences or modelling assumptions. They are derived from sourced endpoints, from a sister species, or from general conifer morphology.
- **n.s.** means no source was found.

Time is given as *t*, months after pollination. Fractions of mature length were computed from the cited ranges, so they inherit those ranges' uncertainty.

A few source conflicts matter for modelling and are flagged where they arise. Norway spruce receptive length is given as about 6 mm by one source and 5 cm by another. Cedar of Lebanon's cycle is put at 17–18 months by the WPSM and at about 24 months by a 2026 Taurus field study. Noble fir mature length in the FEIS appears with a likely units error ("11 to 18 inches"), and that figure is excluded here ([FEIS, Abies procera](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/tree/abipro/all.html)).

## No source describes a flat base on any young cone

The base question is best answered across species first, because the evidence converges even where individual species are thinly documented.

### Young bases taper into stalks or collars

At the earliest stage the cone is a small body grading into its attachment.

- **Douglas fir:** female strobili become receptive once they have "emerged half to two-thirds from the bud scales". At pollination the base of the 3 cm cone therefore sits inside a cup of bud scales rather than presenting a face of its own ([WPSM Pseudotsuga](https://www.fs.usda.gov/nsl/Wpsm/Pseudotsuga.pdf)).
- **Giant sequoia:** the receptive conelet is "only two or three times as large in diameter as the twigs bearing them" ([USDA Silvics, giant sequoia](https://research.fs.usda.gov/silvics/giant-sequoia)). Its base cannot be much wider than its stalk.
- **Scots pine:** "egg-shaped" both as a 6–10 mm receptive strobilus and as a 13 mm first-year conelet "nodding on a short stalk" ([Minnesota Wildflowers, Scots pine](https://www.minnesotawildflowers.info/tree/scots-pine)). Flora of New Zealand records "conelets stalked" ([nzflora, P. sylvestris](https://www.nzflora.info/factsheet/taxon/Pinus-sylvestris.html)).
- **Merkus pine:** described as "oblong-conical with a rounded base before opening", on a stout peduncle of about 1 cm ([Gymnosperm Database, P. merkusii](https://conifers.org/pi/Pinus_merkusii.php)).
- **Japanese larch:** cones sit "on short, curved, bumpy stalks" at short-shoot tips ([Gymnosperm Database, L. kaempferi](https://www.conifers.org/pi/Larix_kaempferi.php)).
- **Cedar of Lebanon:** cones are "more or less sessile" on dwarf shoots ([Trees and Shrubs Online, C. libani](https://www.treesandshrubsonline.org/articles/cedrus/cedrus-libani/)).
- **Noble fir:** cones are borne on the upper side of one-year twigs ([Silvics, Abies procera](https://srs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/misc/ag_654/volume_1/abies/procera.htm)), either sessile ([Gymnosperm Database](https://conifers.org/pi/Abies_procera.php)) or short-stalked ([TSO](https://www.treesandshrubsonline.org/articles/abies/abies-procera/)).

### Broad bases are documented only on mature or opened cones

Every sourced description of a broad or asymmetric base belongs to a full-size or opened cone:

- **Lodgepole pine:** apophyses are "often mammillate toward outer cone base" and the base is "variably asymmetric". The cone is "lanceoloid to ovoid before opening" and only "broadly ovoid to globose when open" ([Gymnosperm Database mirror, FNA](https://www-archiv.fdm.uni-hamburg.de/b-online/earle/pi/pin/cont.htm)).
- **Chir pine:** closed mature cones are "5–8 cm broad at the base" ([Wikipedia, P. roxburghii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinus_roxburghii)).
- **Merkus pine:** becomes "broad-ovoid when fully opened" ([Gymnosperm Database](https://conifers.org/pi/Pinus_merkusii.php)), roughly twice as thick as when closed ([ICRAF](https://apps.worldagroforestry.org/treedb2/speciesprofile.php?Spid=18090)).
- **Cedar of Lebanon:** cones are "barrel-shaped in the year before they swell and mature, then become much more rounded as they ripen" ([TSO, C. libani](https://www.treesandshrubsonline.org/articles/cedrus/cedrus-libani/)). Diameter therefore grows late.

The inference is that base breadth tracks apophysis swelling and scale opening. Both are near zero in a conelet and develop during the final enlargement and lignification.

### Likely cause of the bug and a fix (inference)

A flat young base most plausibly comes from scaling a mature radius profile uniformly, so the big basal shoulder survives in miniature. A more faithful approach makes the base radius a function of development stage:

- Hold the stalk or collar radius roughly constant in absolute size.
- Give the young cone an ovoid or ellipsoid profile tapering at both ends, with base radius close to stalk radius. For sequoia, the sourced 2–3× twig ratio implies a base radius about one-third to one-half of the cone's maximum radius.
- Let basal-apophysis thickness grow from near zero to its mature value only during the species' final growth phase.

General pine morphology (not specifically sourced here) also has the lowest scales small and sterile. That supports tapering scale size toward both ends of the axis at every stage.

## Five growth schedules decide what "young" means

The 13 species fall into five developmental schedules. A generator should key its stage parameter to these schedules rather than to a shared timeline.

**Single-season cones (Douglas fir, noble fir, Norway spruce, Japanese larch).** The cone bud is initiated the summer before, but the cone itself is pollinated in spring and matures that autumn.

- **Douglas fir** is fertilized "about 10 weeks after pollination" and ripens in August to early September ([WPSM Pseudotsuga](https://www.fs.usda.gov/nsl/Wpsm/Pseudotsuga.pdf)).
- **Noble fir** growth is "generally completed by mid-August" ([Silvics, A. procera](https://srs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/misc/ag_654/volume_1/abies/procera.htm)).
- **Norway spruce** cones turn brown 5–7 months after pollination ([Wikipedia, Picea abies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picea_abies)). The analogue species *P. glauca* reaches maximum cone size at about 800 growing degree days, roughly mid-summer ([Wikipedia, P. glauca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picea_glauca)).

In this group the young phase is compressed into a few weeks, and the receptive cone is already a sizeable fraction of final length. For Douglas fir that fraction is 30–50% on the coast.

**Two-year pines (lodgepole, sugar, Scots, chir, Merkus).** The conelet stays small and closed through year one, then enlarges after fertilization at about 12 months.

- **Sugar pine** is 2.5–5 cm at pollination and "may roughly double in size by the end of that growing season". Fertilization follows about 12 months after pollination ([FEIS, P. lambertiana](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/tree/pinlam/all.html)). Against a 30 cm average mature cone, that leaves about two-thirds or more of the length to year two.
- **Scots pine** goes from 6–10 mm at receptivity to about 13 mm at the end of year one, against 38–64 mm at maturity ([Minnesota Wildflowers](https://www.minnesotawildflowers.info/tree/scots-pine)).

**Three-year stone pine.** Stone pine extends the pine pattern by a year. The conelet "lignifies and grows slightly, to about 1 cm diameter in the first year and about 2 cm in the second" ([Valdiviesso et al. 2017](https://om.ciheam.org/ressources/om/pdf/a122/00007243.pdf)). "Most cone growth happens from March to June of the year before harvest" ([Freire et al. 2019](https://www.mdpi.com/1999-4907/10/2/169/html)).

**Front-loaded sequoia.** Giant sequoia inverts the pine pattern. "Fertilization usually occurs in August, by which time cones are almost full-size" ([USDA Silvics](https://research.fs.usda.gov/silvics/giant-sequoia)). Cones are "more than three-quarters their full size" at the end of the first season ([Hartesveldt et al. 1975](https://www.npshistory.com/series/science/hartesveldt/chap5.htm)).

**Autumn-pollinated cedar and the poorly documented bunya.**

- **Cedar of Lebanon** is pollinated in autumn and overwinters small. It reaches 2–3 cm the next summer and about 8.5 cm in the second autumn after flowering ([Frontiers in Forests and Global Change 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/forests-and-global-change/articles/10.3389/ffgc.2026.1840736/pdf)).
- **Bunya pine** matures "about 17 months after pollination" ([Gymnosperm Database, A. bidwillii](https://mail.conifers.org/ar/Araucaria_bidwillii.php)). No source describes its intermediate sizes.

The receptive Douglas fir strobilus is a spiky cluster of forward-pointing tridents in red or green, half-hidden in bud scales. A sourced pest-timing sequence then turns it from erect through horizontal to pendant within the weeks after pollen shed ([FGC BC](https://forestgeneticsbc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/2-Douglas-Fir-Cone-Gall-Midge-Contarinia-oregonensis.pdf)).

A cone-bagging study gives an indirect time bound. It started 26 days after peak receptivity and ran 117 days, which brackets the season at about 20 weeks ([OSU CIPS](https://archive-cips.forestry.oregonstate.edu/sites/cips/files/Reproduction.pdf)).

The relative bract exsertion is an inference, though a well-grounded one. Bracts dominate at receptivity, while mature bracts merely protrude beyond much larger scales, so bract length as a fraction of cone length should fall steadily from receptivity onward.

Noble fir is the least documented of the single-season cones at the receptive stage. No source gives its size or bract posture at pollination. The young-to-full-size transition therefore fits into roughly ten weeks. The base sits sessile or near-sessile on the upper surface of the twig. A rounded base hugging the twig is the safer assumption, since no source describes a truncate one.

Larch shows the clearest reversal of bract dominance in the dataset. At pollination the bracts curl back over the cone and hide the scales. By summer the green seed scales have overgrown them. Fixed 6–7 mm bracts against scales growing to 10–13 mm give a sourced basis for shrinking relative exsertion. A Japanese naturalist source reports that cones "mostly hang downward as they mature", which conflicts with the floras ([kinomemocho](https://kinomemocho.com/sanpo_cone..html)). The likeliest explanation is drooping branchlets rather than a change in cone-to-shoot angle. The rose-like recurved scales are a maturity and drying feature and should not appear on green cones.

Lodgepole is the clearest case where a broad, lopsided base is real but late. Placing full size at about months 13–14 is an inference from general hard-pine development.

## Full size freezes outline, scale count and attachment

Across all 13 species, the sources support the user's plan to leave middle-to-late morphology alone, with one redefinition: freeze geometry at the moment each species reaches full size.

### What is fixed once full size is reached

For the single-season cones the freeze point is sourced or nearly so:

- **Noble fir:** growth is "generally completed by mid-August", followed only by ripening and shattering ([Silvics](https://srs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/misc/ag_654/volume_1/abies/procera.htm)).
- **Douglas fir:** late-stage changes documented in the sources are browning, which starts with the bracts, and scales reflexing on drying ([WPSM](https://www.fs.usda.gov/nsl/Wpsm/Pseudotsuga.pdf); [UW ESRM](https://courses.washington.edu/esrm412/protocols/2008/PSME.pdf)).

For sequoia, scale number and spiral are documented with unusual precision: a mean of 33.5 scales, and a 3/5 phyllotaxis in "several thousand" cones examined ([Harvey et al. 1980](https://npshistory.com/series/science/12/chap5.htm)).

That scale count and parastichy are fixed at initiation in every species is standard conifer morphology rather than a sourced finding here. It is still the strongest justification for keeping scale count and spiral constant across stages and changing only scale size and shape.

Attachment type is constant in kind for every species: sessile, short-stalked, peduncled or bud-collared. Sugar pine's lengthening peduncle and sequoia's thickening stalk are the only documented or strongly implied changes in degree. Orientation is fixed after an early turn in three species:

- **Douglas fir** is pendant within weeks of pollination.
- **Norway spruce** becomes pendent "after pollination" and hangs by late August.
- **Scots pine** is nodding by the end of year 1.

Noble fir, cedar, larch and bunya stay erect throughout.

### What still changes after full size

Five things still change after full size, and none of them alters outline or scale count:

- **Colour** changes in every species.
- **Lignification and gloss** develop, as in lodgepole's "shiny yellowish-brown" pre-mature apophyses.
- **Scales open or reflex**, as in Douglas fir, larch and the opening pines.
- **Some cones disintegrate:** noble fir, cedar and bunya.
- **Old sequoia cones** become knobby, bulbous and lichen-covered while staying green for 8–20+ years ([Hartesveldt 1975](https://www.npshistory.com/series/science/hartesveldt/chap5.htm)).

### Where the freeze rule needs care

The redefinition matters most for stone pine, cedar and the two-year pines. For stone pine, a cone at half its maturation period is still a 20 mm sphere. For cedar, a cone at the same point is still a slim green barrel. For the two-year pines, the apophysis relief that defines the mature look (lodgepole mammillae, chir hooks, the Merkus keel) forms only in the final growth phase. A calendar-fraction stage parameter would push these immature forms into what the generator treats as "middle" stages. A full-size-fraction parameter avoids that.

## Conclusion

The flat-base artefact is less a missing detail than a category error. Mature-cone bases are broad because of features that develop late: swollen basal apophyses, open scales, and a cone barrel that has spread against its shoot. Every early-stage description in the sources points to a tapered or rounded body whose base is set by its stalk, collar or rosette.

The deeper implication for the generator is that one normalised "age" parameter cannot serve all 13 species. Sequoia is nearly full-size at a fifth of its cycle. Stone pine sits under a fifth of its final length for two years. Cedar slims and then fattens. A stage variable defined as "fraction of the way to full size", with species-specific mappings to calendar time and with base radius and apophysis relief tied to that variable, keeps the existing middle-to-late geometry untouched while making early stages credible.

The remaining uncertainty is concentrated in a few places, and closing it would mean retrieving specific monographs rather than doing more general searching. The weak points are bunya, chir pine and noble fir at the receptive stage, the shape of every growth curve between the sourced endpoints, and the orientation of the young pine conelets. The monographs to retrieve are Allen & Owens on Douglas fir, Owens & Molder on lodgepole, Sarvas on Scots pine and Norway spruce, Mutke on stone pine, Buchholz on sequoia, and Sharma on chir pine.

What the map shows

Each 30 m cell gets a relative risk index from 0 to 100. It combines how likely an avalanche is to start there or reach there (terrain), how unstable the snowpack is (conditions), and how bad the outcome would be (consequence). It is a comparison tool, not a probability.

The map is a 3D model of the elevation data, drawn as a 60 m mesh with no vertical exaggeration. The risk or ATES layer, 100 m contours and hillshade are draped over it at 15 m detail.

Terrain layers (pre-computed)

  • Elevation: USGS 3DEP 1 arc-second DEM, resampled to a 30 m grid over about 17 × 20.5 km covering Whistler, Blackcomb, the Spearhead Range and Wedgemount.
  • Release areas: fuzzy slope membership peaking near 40° (about 0.5 at 29° and 51°), reduced on very rough terrain and below treeline.
  • Runout exposure: energy-line propagation downhill from release areas using a 24° travel angle above treeline and 28° below (forest friction proxy). This catches valley floors and lakeshores under big slopes.
  • Terrain traps: gullies (concave curvature), cliffs (>50°), and abrupt flats below steep slopes.
  • ATES class: the three classes of the Parks Canada Avalanche Terrain Exposure Scale (technical model v1-04): Simple, Challenging, Complex. ATES rates terrain at route scale, so each 30 m cell is rated from the terrain within about 150 m. The scale's italic "default" descriptors force a class or higher:
    • Challenging: isolated slopes over 35° (over 2 % of nearby cells), isolated exposure to start zones and tracks (inside a release area, or runout reach of 0.25 or more).
    • Complex: a large share over 35° (30 % or more of nearby cells), many terrain traps under steep ground (25 % or more trap cells with at least 10 % over 35°), numerous overlapping paths (runout reach of 0.6 or more with 30 % or more release-area cells within about 225 m; strong runout under a single path stays Challenging), and frequent exposure to start zones (inside a 35°+ release area where 30 % or more of nearby cells are release areas).
    • Non-default descriptors: slopes over 30° on the cell, or open terrain above 1500 m with 20 % or more over 30°, also make it Challenging.
    The percentage cut-offs stand in for words like "isolated" and "large %" and are our choices, not values from the scale. Glaciers, crevasses, forest density and avalanche frequency are not mapped, so they cannot raise a class. Result for this map: Simple 41 %, Challenging 31 %, Complex 28 % of cells. A route's highest class is the highest it holds for 60 m or more; the assessment also shows how much of the route is in each class.

Overall danger and rating source

  • The overall danger is the highest of the three elevation-band ratings, shown with the matching icon and travel advice from the North American Public Avalanche Danger Scale. The three bands are listed below it.
  • The switch above the rating chooses Avalanche Canada's published rating or this model's rating. It changes the badge, the heat map and the route scores. When Avalanche Canada has no rating for the day (early or late season, beyond its 3-day forecast, or before 2022) the model's rating is used and marked as modelled.

7-day forecast and trip planner

  • Text forecast and weather warnings: Environment and Climate Change Canada city forecast for Whistler (bc-86). It is written for the village, not the alpine.
  • Numbers by elevation: Environment Canada's GEM model through Open-Meteo, with temperature adjusted to 1100, 1700 and 2100 m (the model's band elevations). Open-Meteo adjusts only the temperature for elevation, so each band's precipitation is split into snow (about 1 cm per mm below 0 °C) and rain (above +2 °C, mixed between), the same rule the snowpack model uses. Freezing level is worked out from the temperatures at the three elevations. Ridgetop wind is the 700 hPa wind (about 3000 m); valley wind is the 10 m wind.
  • Picking a day runs the snowpack model on to that day using the forecast weather, then scores the map and routes for it. Danger for days 2 to 7 is modelled; Avalanche Canada forecasts at most 3 days. Forecast skill drops after about 3 days.
  • Weather animation: particles fall over the 3D map at the selected forecast hour. The number of particles follows the precipitation rate, they are snow above the freezing level and rain below it, and they drift with the ridgetop wind direction and speed. Speeds are exaggerated so the motion can be seen. It is an illustration of one forecast point, not a radar.

Manual weather station entry

  • Readings entered by hand replace the modelled weather for today only: new snow, wind and the temperature (moved to 2100 m at −6.5 °C per km). They do not change the 60-day snowpack history, the weak layers or the modelled danger rating.

Last 60 days of weather

  • For the selected date the app pulls the hourly weather before it (from 1 October of the season) for a point on Whistler Mountain from Open-Meteo: current forecast models for recent dates, archived forecast models from 2022, ERA5 reanalysis before that. ERA5 precipitation is also pulled and averaged with the model, because the two disagreed by about three times in autumn 2026 (21 mm against 62 mm). If today's pull fails it uses the snapshot saved in the page and says so.
  • No station correction: there is no public weather station on Whistler or Blackcomb. The nearest public stations are highway stations in the valley (Brandywine 496 m, Tantalus 320 m). Instead, one overall snowfall multiplier is tuned against Avalanche Canada's ratings in the accuracy test.
  • The Avalanche Canada forecast for the selected date (Sea-to-Sky region) sets the danger ratings and problems when one was issued (archive from 2022). Mountain Information Network reports within 20 km are listed for context but do not change the score.
  • The single weather point is moved to three band elevations (below treeline 1100 m, treeline 1700 m, alpine 2100 m) with a −6.5 °C/km lapse rate and +50 % precipitation per km. Precipitation falls as snow below 0 °C, rain above +2 °C, mixed in between.
  • Each day and band: snow depth (new snow at about 1 cm per mm of water, 3 % daily settlement, degree-day melt), snow available for wind transport, and cold dry days on a thin snowpack (faceting).
  • For each band and each of 8 aspects a day counts as loading when it reaches a threshold: 30 cm storm snow in 72 h, 20 cm wind-deposited snow on lee aspects (wind over 20 km/h moving recent snow), 10 mm rain on a 30 cm snowpack, or strong warming on sunny aspects.
  • Recent loading = strongest loading in the last days, counted 100 % today, 80 % yesterday, 60 % two days ago and so on. It multiplies the hazard on that band and aspect by up to 2.1×. Thin snowpack (under about 20 cm) lowers hazard to about a third, because there is not enough snow to slide.
  • When Avalanche Canada issued no rating (early or late season, or before 2022) the app models one from the replay and marks it as modelled.

Buried weak layers

  • The model runs day by day from 1 October of the season (or 61 days back in summer) so layers buried before the 60-day window are kept.
  • Surface hoar: a clear night (Open-Meteo cloud cover under 20 %), light wind and a cold night at that elevation, with no new snow. Near-surface facets: a clear, cold night with little recent snow. Crusts: rain on snow, or melting on sunny slopes. Facets near the ground: a strong temperature gradient (ground at 0 °C) through a thin, cold snowpack for several days.
  • A layer is buried once enough new snow falls on it, then settles with the snowpack. It counts as a persistent slab problem between two burial depths and as deep persistent beyond, and fades with age or after a warm spell.
  • These rules are fixed, not tuned, to limit overfitting. Surface hoar uses night wind under 10 km/h (2.8 m/s) and night air below −8 °C, and faceting a gradient above 10 °C per m, from published sources (UBC ATSC 113 course notes; a 2026 Copernicus surface hoar study). The burial depths (persistent at 30–100 cm, deep beyond 100 cm), layer life (60 days) and the other thresholds are starting guesses without a source. The layers are modelled, not observed.

Accuracy test

Exposure time

  • Travel time uses the Munter calculation: 1 km of distance or 100 m of climb or descent is one unit, at 4 units an hour uphill and 6 downhill, or half that in deep snow or on snowshoes.
  • Minutes in start zones (release potential above 0.45) and under slopes (runout reach 0.25 or more) are added up. Risk-weighted exposure multiplies each minute by the score at that spot.
  • Warming: on the selected day, when the hourly air temperature at that elevation is above 0 °C and the sun is out, the score on sunny slopes is raised by up to 1.8×. This rule is a simplification; the test checks the daily version against days Avalanche Canada listed wet avalanche problems.

Conditions model

  • Danger rating for each elevation band (BTL < 1500 m, TL 1500–1900 m, ALP > 1900 m, from the earlier Whistler build) scales hazard roughly ×2 per level, following the Avalanche Canada scale.
  • Avalanche problems raise hazard on the listed aspects and bands (likelihood and size) and lower it elsewhere. Persistent and deep persistent problems extend runout weight. Loose problems shorten it. Cornices add hazard on crests.
  • Weather now: new snow loads all aspects above the freezing level. Wind over 15 km/h loads lee aspects near ridge crests. Warming above 0 °C adds hazard on sunny aspects and below the freezing level.

Formula

terrain = 1 − (1 − release) × (1 − 0.85 × runout)
season = (1 + 0.7 × recent loading + 0.35 × facets) × (0.35 + 0.65 × snowpack support)
raw = terrain × danger × problem × weather × season × (1 + 0.6 × trap)
index = 100 × (1 − e^(−1.4 × raw))

Classes: Low < 14, Moderate 14–31, Considerable 31–55, High 55–78, Extreme ≥ 78.

3D print

The STL is built in the browser from the same elevation data at about 0.4 mm spacing, with heights exaggerated 1.5 times. Routes are smooth rounded ridges 2.2 mm tall. The terrain has no risk texture and there is no legend strip. The model is watertight with a flat base.

Route scoring

Routes are sampled every 15 m. Route score = 0.55 × 90th percentile + 0.45 × maximum, so one serious crux can't be averaged away by long safe sections. Cruxes are continuous stretches at Considerable or above.

Safest route

A* search over the 30 m grid. Step cost = distance × slope effort + uphill gain + slider weight × distance × (index/100)². Slopes over 48° are treated as impassable. The search finds the path with the lowest total cost.

Limitations

  • One weather point for the whole map. Terrain spreads it by elevation band and aspect only; local wind channelling between the peaks is not modelled.
  • No snowpack layering. The faceting count is a rough flag, not a stability test, and buried weak layers from before the 60-day window are not seen.
  • The model is tuned against Avalanche Canada's forecasts, not against observed avalanches.
  • No forest-cover data: treeline effects come from elevation bands only.
  • Summer trails are scored on their summer line. Winter travel often takes different lines (for example frozen lakes and the valley floor), which can be drawn or loaded as GPX.
  • Trail, run and lift lines come from OpenStreetMap. Where OSM has no line (for example parts of the Spearhead Traverse) a least-effort terrain path joins the waypoints.
  • 30 m cells miss small features like rolls, cornice edges and short gullies. Glaciers, seracs and crevasses (Blackcomb, Spearhead and Wedgemount glaciers) are not modelled.
  • Archived forecast models go back to 2022. Older dates run on ERA5 reanalysis alone.
